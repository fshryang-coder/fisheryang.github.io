Possible Macroeconomic Consequences of AI in China: A Human Capital Perspective

April 2025 · Yang Yifei
MacroeconomicsAI Development

Abstract

This article investigates whether artificial intelligence can strengthen China’s endogenous human capital formation under conditions of Sino-U.S. technological decoupling. Using an adapted neoclassical growth framework, it argues that AI affects growth through two channels: by raising total factor productivity and by accelerating human capital accumulation. The study highlights two mechanisms in the Chinese context: inward effects, whereby AI reshapes education through personalized learning and AI-integrated higher education; and outward effects, whereby AI reduces reliance on foreign talent and technology through domestic large language models, AI chips, and talent repatriation. The paper concludes that AI is both an outcome of China’s long-term human capital investment and a driver of future innovation and technological self-sufficiency.

Keywords: Artificial Intelligence (AI), Human Capital, Technology Transfer

Introduction

Since the release of OpenAI’s ChatGPT model on November 30, 2022, global financial markets have experienced waves of volatility. This turbulence not only reflects the evolving macroeconomic landscape but also influences it. On January 10, 2025, the Chinese AI company DeepSeek released its AI model, DeepSeek-R1, which sent shockwaves through the U.S. stock market. For example, Nvidia’s share price fell by 17%, wiping out $600 billion in market value — the largest one-day loss in American stock market history.

Amid intensifying Sino-U.S. competition, the United States has placed growing strategic importance on maintaining its technological edge. This has led to a series of export controls and visa restrictions aimed at limiting China’s access to foreign talent and cutting-edge technologies. As a result, unlike previous technological revolutions that were heavily reliant on foreign expertise, China’s recent AI advancements are increasingly domestically driven.

Rather than focusing on AI’s impact on labor displacement or productivity gains in China’s macroeconomy, this paper presents a preliminary study on whether AI can help China cultivate endogenous human capital to sustain long-term economic growth amid geopolitical and technological decoupling. Specifically, it investigates two key dimensions:

Inward Effects: How AI can enhance China’s education system.

Outward Effects: How AI reduces reliance on foreign talent and technology.

The paper’s central hypothesis is that AI is both a result of China’s past investments in human capital and a catalyst for future development — reshaping education and diminishing dependence on international technology transfer.

Theoretical Framework: AI in the Growth Model

According to existing literature, the effects of artificial intelligence on macroeconomics are commonly analyzed using either the neoclassical production function or the task-based model. The logic behind these approaches is twofold: first, AI can alter the relative efficiency of labor and capital in the production process, leading to distributional effects and potential unemployment; second, AI can increase Total Factor Productivity (TFP), thereby enhancing the overall efficiency of transforming inputs into outputs.

This paper adopts the neoclassical framework to examine the role of AI in the Chinese context. Although China remains a developing economy, it has taken the lead in certain high-tech sectors. The Solow growth model provides a useful perspective to analyze how breakthroughs in advanced technology, such as AI, can support long-term economic development, especially after the economy approaches its steady state. This paper proposes a novel interpretation of AI’s macroeconomic impact: AI contributes not only through productivity gains but also by accelerating human capital accumulation.

The adapted production function is expressed as:

Y = A·F(K, L, Z)

  • A (TFP): AI acts as a multiplier, boosting productivity across sectors.
  • Z (Human Capital): Traditionally reliant on education and foreign knowledge spillovers, now increasingly dependent on domestic AI-driven upskilling.
  • K & L: AI may disrupt traditional labor roles while optimizing capital allocation.

Within this framework, AI contributes to economic growth through two primary mechanisms:

  • As a productivity tool — enhancing TFP (A) across sectors.
  • As a developer of human capital — transforming Human Capital (Z) by improving education and innovation capabilities in a more self-sufficient manner.

AI as a Consequence of China’s Development

The success of DeepSeek is not a coincidence but rather a reflection of China’s long-term development trajectory, particularly its sustained investment in strategic human capital. Over the past three decades, the Chinese government has systematically constructed a national ecosystem conducive to technological advancement, laying a strong foundation for the country’s current position in the global AI landscape.

A cornerstone of this strategy has been comprehensive education reform, most notably through initiatives such as Project 985 and Project 211. Launched in the late 1990s and early 2000s, these programs aimed to elevate selected universities to world-class standards, with a strong emphasis on science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM). As a result, China now graduates more engineers and computer scientists than any other country. For example, in 2020, China produced more than four times as many STEM graduates as the United States. This massive talent base has created a robust domestic pipeline capable of supporting large-scale AI research and development. The core team behind DeepSeek consists entirely of scientists and engineers trained at Chinese universities, marking a departure from the past, when leading tech firms in China heavily depended on foreign-educated talent.

Another crucial factor in China’s AI rise has been the return of overseas-trained professionals. Before geopolitical tensions intensified in 2018, a significant number of Chinese students who studied in the United States and Europe returned home, bringing with them cutting-edge knowledge, international research experience, and valuable global networks. These returnees were instrumental in building the early AI ecosystem in China, founding research labs, AI startups, and fostering university-industry collaborations.

Today, during Trump’s administration, with measures such as increased tariffs and stricter technology export controls, there is a new wave of repatriated talent, including ethnic Chinese experts from global industries. These professionals are increasingly returning to China to work in both the public and private sectors, as well as in academia. A prominent example is Sun Nan, a chip expert from Tsinghua University, who returned to China at the beginning of this year. Before it, during his time in the U.S., he contributed to the development of over 50 cutting-edge chips in the past four years.

In conclusion, China’s AI development appears to be a self-reinforcing process. As the country achieves further technological breakthroughs, it not only enhances its global standing but also attracts more high-caliber talent, both domestic and international, which further accelerates its innovation momentum.

AI’s Role in Human Capital Development

Inward Effects: Revolutionizing Education

AI is profoundly transforming China’s domestic education landscape, with far-reaching implications for human capital development and social mobility. AI-powered tools are not only changing how knowledge is delivered but also how skills are acquired, assessed, and aligned with evolving industrial needs.

One of the most significant developments is the emergence of personalized learning platforms, such as Squirrel AI and TAL Education’s adaptive systems. These platforms use machine learning algorithms to analyze student performance data and customize lesson plans in real-time. By dynamically adjusting the difficulty, pace, and content of instruction, such technologies are proving more effective than traditional classroom methods in closing learning gaps.

In higher education, universities across China have launched hundreds of AI-related courses, many of which are integrated with the domestically developed DeepSeek platform. This initiative aims to foster scientific innovation and better align the education system with labor market demands. For the Chinese government, the use of indigenous AI systems carries fewer national security concerns and is easier to monitor, thus serving both technological innovation and policy oversight goals.

Outward Effects: Reducing Reliance on Foreign Technology

Beyond education, AI plays a central role in China’s broader strategy to reduce reliance on foreign technology, particularly in the context of U.S.-led tech decoupling and export controls. AI serves not only as a critical national objective but also as a tool to accelerate technological self-sufficiency.

In addition to DeepSeek, major Chinese technology companies have accelerated the development of large language models (LLMs). Notable examples include Baidu’s Ernie Bot and Alibaba’s Qwen, the latter of which was selected by Apple to support AI features for iPhones sold in China. These domestic LLMs aim to replace banned or restricted services like ChatGPT, enabling Chinese users and developers to remain competitive in natural language processing, translation, and content creation without depending on Western platforms.

AI is also being leveraged as a talent retention mechanism. As geopolitical tensions and visa restrictions limit opportunities for Chinese researchers abroad, domestic AI opportunities are becoming increasingly attractive. Government-supported AI institutes and innovation parks in cities such as Beijing, Shenzhen, and Shanghai offer structured career pathways that encourage top talent to stay and contribute to a more self-sufficient research ecosystem. For instance, in April 2025, Gerald Yin Zheyao, CEO of Advanced Micro-Fabrication Equipment (AMEC), formally renounced his U.S. citizenship and restored his Chinese nationality, reflecting a growing trend of repatriation among high-level experts.

On the hardware and infrastructure front, China is actively constructing a homegrown AI ecosystem. Huawei’s Ascend AI chips, alongside its MindSpore deep learning framework, represent the country’s efforts to replace U.S.-made technologies such as Nvidia’s A100 GPUs and open-source software frameworks like TensorFlow and PyTorch. While these domestic alternatives may currently lag behind in certain performance metrics, they mark critical progress toward reducing systemic vulnerabilities and achieving long-term technological autonomy.

Conclusion

While the success of DeepSeek alone may not represent a definitive turning point in China’s macroeconomic development, it does underscore the growing potential of domestically cultivated talent produced by China’s education system. The steady accumulation of human capital over recent decades has laid a solid foundation for future breakthroughs in advanced technology, which may be further accelerated by the integration of AI.

This research represents only a preliminary exploration into the mechanisms through which AI influences human capital development. As more data becomes available over time, future studies will be better positioned to conduct detailed analyses and empirical evaluations, deepening our understanding of AI’s long-term impact on China’s economic trajectory.
