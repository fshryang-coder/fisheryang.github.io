Research

Selected research projects in political economy, comparative growth, and public-sector labor markets.

Does Education's Effect on Political Participation Vary Across Cultures?

Capstone Project, SAIS  ·  Supervisor: Prof. Filipe Campante  ·  Nov. 2025 – May 2026

Question. Does the effect of education on political participation vary across cultural backgrounds?

Approach. I analyse cross-national individual-level data from the World Values Survey using principal components analysis and a two-stage least squares framework to examine how cultural orientations moderate the education–participation relationship.

Coding. R Studio

Contribution. The project suggests that education's civic returns are not uniform: autonomy- and pluralism-oriented cultures strengthen the translation of schooling into participation, while conformity- and productivity-oriented cultures weaken it.

GDP Convergence: Empirical Cases from China, the US, Japan, and South Korea

Course Research Project, SAIS  ·  Supervisor: Prof. Jaime Marquez  ·  Aug. 2025 – Nov. 2025

Question. Does the GDP per capita gap between China and the United States show signs of convergence over time, relative to Japan and South Korea?

Approach. I analyse IMF World Economic Outlook data on GDP per capita from 1980–2022 using log-linear convergence regressions, with time-sensitivity and quadratic-trend robustness checks, to examine long-run growth dynamics across China, the United States, Japan, and South Korea.

Coding. R Studio, Stata

Contribution. The project suggests that catching-up economies rely more on capital accumulation, while developed economies depend more on TFP-driven growth; it also shows that convergence can be temporarily disrupted by country-specific shocks but may resume as structural adjustment takes place.

Who Suffers the Most from Government Layoffs: Case Study of San Francisco

Course Research Project, LKYSPP  ·  Supervisor: Prof. Lawrence Jin  ·  Feb. 2025 – Apr. 2025

Question. Which government employees are most vulnerable to layoffs, and how does vulnerability vary across departments and income levels?

Approach. I analyse San Francisco public employee compensation data using departmental comparisons, salary-ratio clustering, and regression models to examine how compensation structures and organizational type shape resilience to layoffs.

Coding. Python

Contribution. The project suggests that vulnerability is unevenly distributed: employees in certain departments and lower-income groups are less resilient to layoffs, highlighting both structural and economic disparities in government workforce cuts.
